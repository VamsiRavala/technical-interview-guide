# Interview Questions

100 detailed Q&A for an AI Solutions Architect loop on a Microsoft-centric stack: **50 AI Architect** questions (platform strategy, cost, governance, reliability) and **50 System Design** questions (design-this-system prompts). Answers are architect-level — explicit trade-offs, named Azure services, and the reasoning a hiring panel wants to hear. Accurate to **mid-2026**.

## AI Architect

### Enterprise AI Platform Design

**Q1.** Sketch the reference architecture for an enterprise GenAI platform on Azure that 30+ product teams will share.

I treat the platform as a paved road, not a one-off app. At the center sits **Azure API Management (APIM)** acting as an AI gateway in front of **Azure OpenAI** deployments, fronting both PTU and PAYG backends. Behind APIM I place a thin orchestration layer (Azure Container Apps or AKS) running .NET services that own prompt assembly, RAG retrieval, and tool calling. Knowledge lives in **Azure AI Search** (hybrid + semantic ranker) with **Cosmos DB** for chat state and **Blob/ADLS Gen2** for source documents. Cross-cutting concerns are platform-owned: identity via **Entra ID** workload identities, secrets in **Key Vault**, telemetry in **Application Insights + Log Analytics**, and content safety via **Azure AI Content Safety**.

The key trade-off is **centralization vs. team autonomy**. I centralize the gateway, observability, quota, and safety (so governance is enforced once) but let teams own their prompts, indexes, and orchestration logic via self-service onboarding. This avoids the anti-pattern of every team provisioning its own Azure OpenAI resource with no cost visibility or shared guardrails. The platform exposes a stable API contract so models can be swapped underneath without breaking consumers.

**Q2.** How do you decide what belongs in the shared platform versus each product team?

I use a "differentiation test": anything that is undifferentiated heavy lifting and risky to get wrong belongs in the platform; anything that encodes product-specific business value belongs to the team. Platform-owned: the AI gateway, authentication, rate limiting and quota, content safety enforcement, model routing, cost attribution, prompt-injection defenses, observability pipelines, and red-team tooling. Team-owned: domain prompts, retrieval indexes, evaluation datasets, agent tool definitions, and UX.

The trade-off is **velocity vs. control**. Push too much into the platform and you become a bottleneck — teams route around you and you lose governance entirely (shadow AI). Push too little and you get inconsistent security and duplicated cost. My rule: the platform must be opt-in-easy and hard-to-bypass. I provide a golden-path SDK (a thin .NET client library wrapping the gateway) so the easy path is also the compliant path. I also publish a capability maturity model so teams know what "good" looks like, and I run the platform as an internal product with an SLA, a roadmap, and consumer feedback loops rather than as a gatekeeping committee.

**Q3.** What does a self-service onboarding flow for a new AI product team look like?

Onboarding should be infrastructure-as-code, not tickets. A team submits a request (Git PR against a config repo) declaring: their tenant/business unit, expected TPM, data classification, and required models. A pipeline (GitHub Actions or Azure DevOps) provisions an APIM **product + subscription key**, a dedicated **Entra ID app registration**, a scoped AI Search index, a Cosmos container, and budget alerts — all via **Bicep/Terraform**. Cost attribution tags are stamped automatically.

The trade-off is **standardization vs. flexibility**. I default everything to secure, governed templates (Private Endpoints on, CMK on, content safety on) and require explicit justification + approval to deviate. This keeps 90% of teams on the paved road with zero friction while allowing the regulated 10% to negotiate exceptions through policy-as-code (Azure Policy + custom OPA-style gates). The output a team receives is a ready-to-use gateway endpoint, a subscription key in their Key Vault, dashboards in Application Insights, and a quickstart .NET sample. The whole flow targets under a day. The failure mode I design against is "onboarding so painful teams build their own thing" — friction is the enemy of governance.

### RAG vs. Fine-Tune vs. Agent Decisions

**Q4.** Walk me through how you decide between RAG, fine-tuning, and an agentic approach.

These solve different problems, so I frame it as a decision tree, not a competition. **RAG** is my default when the need is *knowledge* — answers grounded in current, proprietary, or frequently-changing facts. It gives freshness, citeability, and access control at the document level without retraining. **Fine-tuning** is for *behavior and form* — consistent tone, structured output, domain jargon, or compressing a long system prompt to cut latency and tokens. It does not teach new facts reliably and it ages as the base model updates. **Agents** are for *multi-step tasks* requiring tool use, planning, and acting against systems — booking, querying APIs, orchestrating workflows.

The real-world answer is usually a combination: RAG for grounding, a light fine-tune (or just good prompting) for format, wrapped in an agent loop when actions are required. The dominant trade-offs are cost, latency, and maintainability. Fine-tuning adds MLOps burden and re-train cycles; agents add latency, unpredictability, and a larger attack/failure surface. On Azure I start with RAG over **AI Search** because it is the cheapest to operate and easiest to evaluate, and only escalate to fine-tuning or agents when evidence (evals) shows RAG alone cannot meet the requirement.

**Q5.** When is fine-tuning genuinely worth it on Azure OpenAI, and what's the hidden cost?

Fine-tuning earns its place when you have (1) a stable, repetitive task, (2) hundreds-to-thousands of high-quality examples, and (3) a measurable gap that prompting plus RAG cannot close — typically rigid output formatting, classification at scale, or aggressive prompt compression for latency/cost. On Azure OpenAI you pay for training tokens *and* an hourly hosting charge for the deployed fine-tuned model whether or not it is used, which is a meaningful fixed cost that PAYG inference does not carry.

The hidden cost is lifecycle. A fine-tune is frozen against a base model version; when Azure deprecates or upgrades that base, you re-train and re-validate. You also need a versioned dataset, an eval harness, and a rollback story — this is real MLOps, not a one-time job. My opinionated stance: most teams reach for fine-tuning prematurely. I push them to exhaust few-shot prompting, structured outputs / JSON mode, and RAG first because those are reversible and cheap to iterate. I reserve fine-tuning for the narrow cases where the ROI — usually token savings at very high volume or accuracy on a closed task — clearly clears the added operational tax.

**Q6.** How do you keep an agentic system from spiraling — runaway loops, cost, and hallucinated tool calls?

Agents are powerful and dangerous because they convert a single request into an unbounded sequence of model + tool calls. I bound them on three axes. **Step/iteration limits**: a hard max on reasoning loops and a token budget per task, enforced in the orchestrator, not the prompt. **Cost ceilings**: per-request and per-session spend caps tracked against the gateway, killing the run when exceeded. **Tool contracts**: every tool has a strict schema, input validation, idempotency keys for mutating actions, and a human-in-the-loop gate for high-risk operations (payments, deletes, external sends).

The trade-off is **autonomy vs. safety/cost predictability**. More autonomy improves task success but worsens worst-case cost and blast radius. I lean conservative: deterministic guardrails around a non-deterministic core. On Azure, **Semantic Kernel** or the **Azure AI Agent Service** gives me a structured orchestration surface where I can inject these limits, log every step to App Insights, and replay traces. I also separate "planner" calls (cheaper/faster model) from "executor" calls and require tool outputs to be re-grounded before they are trusted, which curbs the agent acting on hallucinated intermediate results.

### Multi-Tenant AI Isolation

**Q7.** How do you architect tenant isolation for a multi-tenant SaaS built on Azure OpenAI?

Isolation must hold across four planes: data, compute/quota, identity, and prompt context. For **data**, I never share a vector index across tenants without enforced filtering. I either give each tenant its own AI Search index (strong isolation, higher cost/ops at scale) or use a shared index with a mandatory `tenantId` security filter applied server-side — never trusting the client. For **identity**, each tenant maps to an Entra ID-scoped principal; the gateway resolves tenant from the validated token, not from a request body field. For **quota**, APIM enforces per-tenant rate and token limits so one noisy tenant cannot starve others.

The core trade-off is **isolation strength vs. cost and operational scale**. Index-per-tenant (silo) is the gold standard for regulated customers but explodes management overhead past a few hundred tenants; a pooled model with rigorous row-level filters scales but concentrates the risk in your filter logic — a single bug leaks cross-tenant data. My pragmatic approach is **tiered**: pooled for standard tenants, dedicated indexes (and even dedicated PTU deployments / CMK) for enterprise tenants who pay for and require it. Critically, the security filter is injected by the platform, validated, and impossible for tenant code to override.

**Q8.** A pooled vector index leaked one tenant's data into another's results. How would you have prevented it, and how do you respond?

Prevention is defense in depth. First, the tenant filter is applied by the trusted retrieval service from the authenticated identity — the application layer never accepts a tenant ID from the caller. Second, I tag every chunk at ingestion with its tenant and run automated isolation tests in CI: synthetic cross-tenant queries that must return zero foreign documents. Third, I'd consider AI Search **security trimming** wired to group membership so retrieval is filtered before ranking, not after.

Response is incident management. Contain immediately (disable the affected query path), assess scope from gateway and search logs (which tenants, which documents, what was surfaced to the model and the user), then notify per contractual and regulatory obligations. The trade-off I'd highlight in the postmortem: the team likely chose a pooled index for cost, which is defensible, but isolation that depends on a single application-layer filter is fragile. I'd add a structural backstop — partition keys, separate indexes for sensitive tenants, or field-level filters enforced in the index definition — so isolation no longer rests on one code path being correct. The cultural fix: cross-tenant leak tests become a release gate.

**Q9.** How do you handle per-tenant customization without operational explosion?

I separate the customization *surface* from the *runtime*. Tenants customize via declarative configuration — prompt templates, allowed tools, retrieval scope, model tier, branding — stored as versioned config (Cosmos DB or a config service), not as forked code or forked infrastructure. The single multi-tenant runtime reads that config at request time keyed by the authenticated tenant. This keeps one codebase and one deployment while letting each tenant feel bespoke.

The trade-off is **flexibility vs. blast radius and testability**. Config-driven customization is cheap to scale but every config knob is a code path you must test across tenants; deep per-tenant logic becomes a combinatorial nightmare. I draw a hard line: anything expressible as data stays config; anything requiring custom code is either generalized into a platform feature or pushed into a tenant-owned extension via a well-defined plugin contract (e.g., a registered tool/function). For premium tenants needing true isolation, I escalate them to dedicated resources — separate PTU deployment, dedicated index, CMK — rather than bending the shared runtime. The governing principle: customization should never compromise the ability to ship a single change safely to all tenants.

### Cost Architecture

**Q10.** How do you architect cost control across PTU, PAYG, caching, and model routing?

Cost is an architecture concern, not a billing afterthought. I build a layered strategy. **Provisioned Throughput Units (PTU)** for the steady, predictable baseline of high-volume production traffic — you reserve capacity at a fixed cost and get latency guarantees, so it is cheaper per token at high, consistent utilization. **Pay-as-you-go (PAYG)** for spiky, unpredictable, or low-volume workloads and as overflow/spillover beyond PTU capacity. **Caching** removes cost entirely for repeated work: prompt caching for repeated long system prompts/context, plus an application-level semantic cache (embed the query, serve a stored answer on a near-match) for FAQ-style traffic. **Model routing** sends each request to the cheapest model that can satisfy it.

The trade-off is **commitment vs. elasticity**: PTU is cheaper only if you keep it busy — under-utilized PTU is wasted money, while pure PAYG at scale is expensive and rate-limited. My pattern is **PTU baseline + PAYG burst**, governed by the gateway, with caching layered on top to shrink the billable volume before either backend is hit. I instrument token cost per tenant/feature so optimization is data-driven, not guesswork.

**Q11.** Explain the PTU-vs-PAYG break-even analysis you'd present to a CFO.

I frame it as utilization economics. PTU is a fixed monthly reservation that buys a guaranteed throughput envelope and predictable latency; PAYG bills per token with no commitment but variable cost and shared-capacity rate limits. The break-even is the utilization point where the PTU monthly cost divided by tokens actually served equals the PAYG per-token rate. Below that utilization, PTU is overpriced idle capacity; above it, PTU is strictly cheaper *and* gives latency SLAs PAYG can't.

```text
PTU effective $/token = PTU monthly cost / tokens served per month
If PTU effective $/token < PAYG $/token  -> PTU wins
```

So the question I ask the CFO is not "PTU or PAYG?" but "what is our sustained, floor-level token volume?" That predictable floor goes on PTU (committed for a discount via reservations); everything above the floor — peaks, experiments, new features — stays PAYG. The trade-offs to name explicitly: PTU adds capacity-planning discipline and the risk of paying for idle units if traffic dips, but removes rate-limit throttling and gives the deterministic latency that customer-facing SLAs need. I recommend starting PAYG, measuring 4-6 weeks of real traffic, then converting the stable baseline to PTU once the floor is proven — never buying PTU on a forecast alone.

**Q12.** How does semantic caching work and where does it bite you?

Semantic caching stores prior prompt/response pairs keyed by the *embedding* of the input, not an exact string. On a new request I embed it, do a vector similarity lookup, and if the nearest cached entry exceeds a similarity threshold I return the cached answer — skipping the LLM call entirely. This can eliminate a large fraction of cost and latency for repetitive, FAQ-shaped traffic. Azure API Management ships **GenAI caching policies** that integrate this at the gateway with a vector store (e.g., Azure Cache for Redis or AI Search) so it works for any backend service uniformly.

Where it bites: the **similarity threshold is a precision/recall dial**. Too loose and you serve a confidently wrong answer to a subtly different question — a correctness and trust failure. Too tight and your hit rate collapses and you've added embedding cost for little benefit. It also poisons easily with personalized or time-sensitive answers ("what's my account balance?" must never be cached cross-user). So I scope caches per tenant/user where appropriate, exclude dynamic and authenticated-data prompts, set conservative thresholds, attach TTLs, and monitor cache-hit *quality* via evals — not just hit rate. The trade-off is always cost savings vs. the risk of confidently stale or mismatched responses.

**Q13.** Design a model-routing strategy that minimizes cost without tanking quality.

I route on request difficulty. A lightweight classifier (or heuristics + a small model) inspects the incoming request and assigns it to a tier: cheap small models (e.g., GPT-4o-mini class) for simple extraction, classification, and short factual tasks; mid-tier for standard reasoning; premium frontier models only for complex reasoning, long context, or high-stakes outputs. The router lives in the gateway/orchestrator so the policy is centralized and auditable. I pair this with **cascade/fallback**: try the cheap model first, and only escalate to a stronger model when a confidence or validation check fails.

The trade-offs are sharp. Routing introduces an extra classification hop (latency + a small cost) and a new failure mode — *misrouting* a hard problem to a weak model degrades quality silently. So I require evals per route to prove the small model is actually sufficient for the traffic I send it, and I monitor escalation rates as a health signal. I also avoid over-engineering: for many products, picking one good default model is fine, and routing only pays off at volume or with clearly bimodal traffic. The opinionated take: start with one model, add routing when telemetry shows a large, separable population of cheap-to-serve requests.

**Q14.** How do you give finance per-feature, per-tenant cost visibility?

You cannot optimize what you cannot attribute. Every request through the gateway carries dimensions — tenant ID, feature/use-case, environment, model — and the gateway logs prompt and completion token counts per call to **Log Analytics**. From there I compute cost by multiplying tokens by the model's rate (or amortizing PTU cost across served tokens) and surface it in **Cost Management** dashboards and Power BI. Resource tagging plus per-tenant APIM subscriptions reinforces the attribution at the Azure billing layer.

The trade-off is **granularity vs. overhead**: logging full token detail per request is rich but adds telemetry volume and cost, and PTU amortization is an allocation model, not a true marginal cost, so it can mislead if a tenant's traffic is bursty. I'm explicit with finance that PTU spend is fixed and should be allocated by share-of-usage, while PAYG is directly attributable. I set per-tenant and per-feature **budgets with alerts** so anomalies (a runaway agent, a prompt-injection abuse spike) trigger before the bill does. The cultural win is making each team see its own AI spend — that single piece of visibility drives more optimization than any clever caching policy.

### Scalability & Throughput

**Q15.** How do you scale a GenAI workload to tens of thousands of concurrent users?

The constraint is rarely your compute — it's the **token-per-minute (TPM) and request-per-minute (RPM) quotas** on the model backend. So scaling is about managing that scarce throughput. I provision PTU for guaranteed capacity, distribute load across **multiple Azure OpenAI deployments and regions** behind the gateway, and use APIM's **load-balancing and circuit-breaker** features to spread traffic and route around throttled backends. The stateless orchestration tier (Container Apps/AKS) scales horizontally and cheaply; the model backend is the bottleneck I architect around.

Key trade-offs: **synchronous vs. asynchronous**. For high concurrency I push non-interactive work (batch summarization, document processing) onto a queue (**Service Bus**) with worker pools that drain at the rate the backend allows, smoothing spikes instead of failing them. Interactive chat uses **streaming responses** so perceived latency stays low and connections free up token-by-token. I also reduce demand: caching, model routing to smaller models, and prompt trimming all cut the TPM I need to buy. The honest constraint to state: you scale GenAI by *managing demand against a hard throughput ceiling*, using queues for elasticity and multi-deployment spillover for headroom — not by naively autoscaling stateless pods.

**Q16.** Walk through handling 429 throttling gracefully.

A 429 from Azure OpenAI means I've hit TPM/RPM limits — it's expected at scale, not exceptional. The first layer is the gateway: APIM detects throttling and **load-balances to a backup deployment** (another region or a PAYG spillover behind a PTU primary) via a priority-ordered backend pool, with a **circuit breaker** that trips a backend out of rotation when it's saturated and re-probes it later. Within the client, I implement **exponential backoff with jitter**, honoring the `Retry-After` header rather than hammering.

The trade-offs: retries add latency and can amplify load if everyone retries simultaneously (the thundering-herd problem — jitter mitigates this). Spillover to PAYG protects availability but at higher per-token cost, so it's a deliberate cost-for-reliability trade. For non-interactive work, the better answer than retrying is **shedding into a queue** and processing at the sustainable rate. I also degrade gracefully: if all backends are saturated, return a smaller/cached result or a clear "try again" rather than a hard error or an indefinite hang. The architecture I describe never lets a 429 reach the user as a raw failure — it's absorbed by routing, backoff, spillover, or queueing, with the user-facing degradation chosen deliberately.

**Q17.** Streaming vs. batch — when and why?

Streaming (token-by-token via SSE) is for **interactive, latency-perceptible** experiences — chat, copilots, anything a human is waiting on. It dramatically improves *perceived* latency because first-token-time is short even when total generation is long, and it frees the user to start reading. Batch processing — including Azure OpenAI's **Batch API** — is for **high-volume, latency-tolerant** work: bulk document classification, embedding generation, offline summarization. The Batch API trades turnaround time (results within a window, not seconds) for a substantial per-token discount and higher effective throughput against a separate quota pool.

The trade-offs: streaming complicates the stack — you need SSE/WebSocket plumbing, partial-response handling, and content-safety scanning on a stream (you can't fully moderate output you haven't finished generating, so you scan incrementally and may have to retract). Batch is operationally simpler and cheaper but useless for anything synchronous. My rule: if a human is staring at a spinner, stream; if a job can complete within minutes-to-hours, batch it for the cost and quota win. Mixing both in one platform is normal — interactive paths stream, ingestion/pipelines batch — and the gateway routes accordingly.

### Reliability, Fallback & Graceful Degradation

**Q18.** Design a fallback chain for when your primary model or region is down.

I design tiered fallback, from cheapest-to-implement to most-degraded. Tier 1: **multi-region, multi-deployment routing** at the gateway — if the primary Azure OpenAI deployment in one region throttles or fails, APIM fails over to a secondary region or a PAYG deployment automatically. Tier 2: **model fallback** — if the preferred model is unavailable, drop to an equivalent or smaller model with adjusted expectations. Tier 3: **cached or retrieved answer** — serve a semantically cached response or, for RAG, return the retrieved source passages with a "summarized answer unavailable" notice so the user still gets value. Tier 4: **honest graceful failure** — a clear message and a queued retry, never a hung request or a stack trace.

The trade-offs are availability vs. consistency and cost. Failing over to a different model can change answer style or quality, which matters for some products and not others; I make that a per-use-case decision. Spillover to PAYG or a second region costs more and must be capacity-planned in advance. Crucially, fallback logic lives in the platform, tested with **chaos/fault injection** so it actually works under real failure — untested fallback is a false sense of safety. The principle: degrade in steps, each step still delivering *some* value, with the user never left staring at a hard error.

**Q19.** How do you achieve graceful degradation when the only available model is dumber or slower?

Graceful degradation means the experience bends instead of breaking. If I must fall back to a smaller/slower model, I adapt the *task* to the model: simplify the prompt, narrow the scope, lower the max tokens, or switch from "generate a full answer" to "return the top retrieved passages with a brief note." For agents, I can disable the riskier autonomous actions and revert to a constrained, human-confirmed flow. The user is informed transparently when output quality may be reduced — silent degradation erodes trust worse than an honest banner.

The trade-offs: degraded modes need their own evals and prompts (a small model often needs a *different* prompt than the frontier one, not the same prompt downgraded), which is extra build and maintenance cost. There's also a product decision about whether a degraded answer is better than no answer — for a creative brainstorming tool, yes; for a medical or financial summary, a refusal may be safer than a low-quality response. I encode that as a per-use-case policy. Architecturally, the degradation paths are first-class, feature-flagged, and load-tested, so when the primary path fails the fallback isn't a cold, untested code path discovered in a 2 a.m. incident.

**Q20.** What's your circuit breaker strategy across model backends?

A circuit breaker prevents a struggling backend from dragging down the whole system and gives it room to recover. At the gateway, each Azure OpenAI backend in the pool has a breaker that trips on a threshold of failures/429s within a window, removing it from rotation; traffic shifts to healthy backends, and after a cooldown the breaker half-opens to probe before fully restoring. APIM's native **backend circuit breaker** and load-balanced pool support this declaratively. This pairs with retries-with-backoff and prioritized failover (PTU primary, PAYG secondary).

The trade-offs: a too-sensitive breaker trips on transient blips and needlessly reduces capacity (and may push you onto more expensive spillover); a too-lax breaker keeps routing to a dying backend, amplifying user-facing errors. I tune the thresholds against observed failure patterns and alert when a breaker trips so humans know capacity is degraded. The subtle architectural point: circuit breaking only helps if you have *somewhere to fail over to* — it's meaningless with a single deployment. So it's part of a broader multi-deployment, multi-region topology, and I size the secondaries to actually absorb shifted load rather than to instantly trip their own breakers in a cascade.

### Security & Data Isolation

**Q21.** How do you secure an Azure OpenAI deployment end to end?

Defense in depth across network, identity, data, and content. **Network**: disable public access and front Azure OpenAI, AI Search, Cosmos, Storage, and Key Vault with **Private Endpoints** so traffic stays on the Microsoft backbone / your VNet; the gateway is the only ingress, protected by **WAF + Azure Front Door**. **Identity**: no API keys in app code — services authenticate with **Entra ID managed identities** and **RBAC** scoped least-privilege; users authenticate via Entra ID and the gateway validates the token. **Data**: secrets in **Key Vault**, encryption with **customer-managed keys (CMK)** for regulated data, and tenant/document-level access trimming in retrieval. **Content**: **Azure AI Content Safety** and Prompt Shields on both input and output, plus prompt-injection defenses.

The trade-offs: Private Endpoints + managed identity add networking and provisioning complexity (DNS, VNet integration) and slow down quick experiments — so I keep a locked-down landing zone but provide IaC templates so security is the default, not a tax teams pay manually. Managed identity over keys is non-negotiable in production; keys are acceptable only in throwaway dev. The theme: identity-based, network-isolated, least-privilege by default, with content safety as a mandatory platform layer no team can disable.

**Q22.** Explain how Entra ID, managed identities, and OBO flow fit an enterprise AI app.

Entra ID is the backbone of identity. End users sign in to the app via Entra ID (OIDC), getting a token the gateway validates and uses to resolve tenant and authorization. The app's *services* never use passwords or keys to reach Azure OpenAI, AI Search, or Storage — they use **managed identities** with Entra **RBAC** roles, so credentials are never stored and rotation is automatic. For RAG that must respect *the user's own* document permissions, I use the **On-Behalf-Of (OBO) flow**: the app exchanges the user's token for a downstream token so retrieval against, say, SharePoint/Graph returns only documents that user is allowed to see — preserving data-access governance through the AI layer.

The trade-offs: OBO is correct but adds latency and complexity (token exchange, consent, downstream permission checks) and only works where the data source honors per-user identity. The simpler app-identity model is faster but means *the app* sees all data and you must enforce per-user trimming yourself in the index — easier to get wrong. My stance: for high-sensitivity, permission-mirrored knowledge, invest in OBO so the AI inherits existing access controls; for broadly-shared corpora, app identity plus security-trimming filters in AI Search is acceptable. Either way, identity is resolved from validated tokens, never from request payloads.

**Q23.** A regulated customer demands their data never be readable by Microsoft or your ops team. What do you architect?

I combine network isolation, customer-managed keys, and access governance. **Customer-managed keys (CMK)** in their own Key Vault (or Managed HSM) encrypt the data at rest for Azure OpenAI fine-tunes, AI Search, Storage, and Cosmos — so the customer holds the key and can revoke it, rendering data unreadable. **Private Endpoints** keep traffic off the public internet. For "not even your ops team," I enforce **least-privilege RBAC + Privileged Identity Management (just-in-time elevation)**, and for the strongest demands, **Azure Confidential Computing** so data is encrypted even in memory during processing, plus **Customer Lockbox** so any Microsoft support access requires customer approval.

The trade-offs are significant: CMK adds key-lifecycle responsibility (lose/revoke the key and you lose the data — including legitimate access), and confidential computing constrains your SKU/region choices and adds cost and complexity. Per-customer CMK and dedicated resources also push you toward the silo isolation model, raising operational overhead. I also remind stakeholders that Azure OpenAI does **not** use customer prompts/completions to train Microsoft's models — that contractual boundary already addresses a common fear. I right-size: pooled + CMK for most regulated tenants, confidential computing + Lockbox + dedicated deployment for the few who truly require zero-trust against the operator.

**Q24.** How do you defend against prompt injection in a RAG/agent system?

Prompt injection is the defining security risk: untrusted content (a retrieved document, a tool result, a user message) carries instructions that hijack the model. There is no single fix, so I layer. **Input/content controls**: Azure AI Content Safety **Prompt Shields** to detect jailbreak and indirect-injection patterns in both user input and retrieved documents. **Structural separation**: clearly delimit and label untrusted content in the prompt and instruct the model to treat retrieved text as data, not instructions — imperfect but raises the bar. **Least privilege on actions**: the model can *request* a tool call but the orchestrator validates and authorizes it; high-impact actions require human confirmation, so a successful injection can't autonomously wire money. **Output controls**: scan and validate model output before it's rendered or executed.

The trade-offs: aggressive shielding produces false positives that block legitimate requests, hurting UX, and no defense is complete — indirect injection via poisoned documents is an active research problem. So I assume injection *will* sometimes succeed and limit blast radius by constraining what the model is *empowered* to do, sandboxing tool execution, and logging everything for detection. The architect's mindset: treat all model-adjacent text as untrusted, and never let the model's output directly trigger an irreversible action without a validated, authorized gate.

### Governance & Responsible AI

**Q25.** How do you operationalize Responsible AI on an enterprise platform, not just as a slide?

I make it a lifecycle with enforcement points, anchored to Microsoft's Responsible AI standard (fairness, reliability/safety, privacy/security, inclusiveness, transparency, accountability). At **design**: an Impact Assessment for each use case classifying risk and required controls. At **build**: mandatory content safety, grounding for factual claims, and disclosure that the user is talking to AI. At **runtime**: the platform *enforces* the non-negotiables — Content Safety on every call, logging, and abuse monitoring — so RAI isn't optional per team. At **operate**: ongoing evals for quality/safety/bias, incident processes, and human oversight for high-stakes decisions.

The trade-offs: governance friction can slow delivery and, if too heavy, drives teams to shadow AI — so I tier controls by risk (a low-risk internal summarizer needs far less ceremony than a customer-facing decisioning system). I also balance transparency vs. complexity: citations and "I'm not sure" behaviors build trust but require RAG plumbing and calibrated refusal. My opinionated position: RAI succeeds only when the safe path is the *easy* path — baked into the platform SDK and gateway defaults — and when there's a named accountable owner per use case. A governance board that only reviews documents, without runtime enforcement, is theater.

**Q26.** How do you keep a non-deterministic system auditable and compliant (e.g., EU AI Act, sector regs)?

Auditability means I can reconstruct *why* the system produced a given output. I log, per request, the inputs, the resolved prompt and prompt *version*, the model and version, retrieved sources (for RAG grounding/citeability), tool calls, content-safety verdicts, and the final output — with the user/tenant identity — into immutable, access-controlled storage (Log Analytics + archived storage). This gives traceability for an audit or a complaint. For regulated decisions I enforce human-in-the-loop and record the human's action, so accountability is clear.

The trade-offs: comprehensive logging of prompts and outputs collides with privacy — those logs contain personal and sensitive data, so they need CMK, retention limits, RBAC, and PII handling, and you must reconcile "log everything for audit" with "minimize personal data." Non-determinism also means you can't reproduce an exact output by replay, so I capture enough context (seed where supported, model version, full prompt, retrieved context) to *explain* it even if I can't bit-for-bit reproduce it. For the **EU AI Act**, I classify each use case by risk tier and apply the corresponding obligations — transparency, human oversight, documentation, monitoring — and treat model/prompt versions as controlled artifacts. The principle: governance rides on disciplined versioning and structured telemetry, not on hoping you can re-run the model later.

**Q27.** How do you measure and mitigate bias and harmful outputs at platform scale?

Measurement first: I maintain evaluation datasets that probe for biased, unsafe, or unfair outputs across protected attributes and known failure modes, run them in CI and on a schedule using **Azure AI Foundry's evaluation tooling** (groundedness, safety, fairness-oriented metrics) plus targeted **red-teaming** (including the PyRIT-style automated adversarial testing Microsoft promotes). Runtime mitigation is **Azure AI Content Safety** filtering on inputs and outputs at configurable severity, grounding to reduce fabrication, and refusal behaviors for out-of-scope or harmful requests.

The trade-offs are real and worth naming. Safety filtering has a precision/recall cost — turn severity up and you block legitimate content (over-refusal frustrates users and can itself be a fairness issue if it disproportionately blocks certain dialects or topics); turn it down and harmful content slips through. Bias is also context-dependent and not fully solvable by a filter, so I treat it as continuous monitoring, not a one-time certification. At platform scale I centralize the eval harness and safety config so improvements ship once to everyone, and I require teams to register their use case's risk profile so the right severity and human-oversight level is applied. The honest stance: you manage and monitor these risks down to an acceptable, documented level — you don't eliminate them, and pretending otherwise is a red flag.

### Observability & Evaluation at Platform Scale

**Q28.** What does observability look like for a GenAI platform beyond normal APM?

Standard APM (latency, errors, throughput) is necessary but insufficient because the *content* and *quality* of responses matter, not just whether the call returned 200. So I add three GenAI-specific layers on top of **Application Insights / Azure Monitor**. **Token & cost telemetry**: prompt/completion tokens per request, tagged by tenant/feature/model, for cost and capacity. **Quality telemetry**: groundedness, relevance, and safety scores sampled and computed via online evaluation, plus user feedback signals (thumbs up/down, edits, regenerations) as proxy quality metrics. **Trace telemetry**: full request traces — prompt version, retrieved chunks, tool calls, agent steps — using OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions so I can debug *why* an answer was bad, not just that it was slow.

The trade-offs: capturing prompts and outputs is gold for debugging but is sensitive data (privacy, retention, CMK) and high-volume (telemetry cost), so I sample intelligently and redact PII. Online quality eval costs extra model calls, so I run it on a sample, not every request. The architectural point: I instrument at the **gateway** so observability is uniform and automatic for every team, and route to **Azure AI Foundry's tracing/monitoring** for GenAI-native views. Without content-level observability you're flying blind on the only thing that matters — whether the AI is actually helping.

**Q29.** How do you build an evaluation framework for a non-deterministic system in CI/CD?

I treat evals as the unit/integration tests of AI. The framework has a versioned **golden dataset** of representative inputs with expected qualities, and scorers that don't require exact matches: LLM-as-judge for correctness/relevance, deterministic checks for format/schema, groundedness scoring for RAG, and safety scores. On every change to a prompt, model, or retrieval config, the pipeline runs the eval suite and **gates the release on aggregate metrics** (e.g., groundedness must not regress below threshold). Azure AI Foundry's evaluation SDK plugs into this, and I track scores over time to catch drift.

The trade-offs: LLM-as-judge is itself non-deterministic and can be biased or inconsistent, so I calibrate judges against human labels and use multiple samples; eval runs cost model calls and time, so I balance suite size against CI speed (smoke evals on every PR, full suite nightly/pre-release). Golden datasets rot — they must be curated continuously and expanded from real failures, or they stop reflecting production. My opinionated take: you cannot iterate safely on prompts or swap models without an eval harness — "it looked good in the demo" is how teams ship regressions. The eval suite is the safety net that makes a non-deterministic system shippable with confidence, and it's a platform asset I provide so every team isn't reinventing it.

**Q30.** How do you detect and respond to quality drift in production?

Drift comes from base-model updates, changing user behavior, stale retrieval data, or a prompt edit. I detect it with continuous signals: a rolling **online eval** on a sampled slice of real traffic (groundedness, relevance, safety), implicit user signals (regenerate rate, thumbs-down, edit distance on accepted outputs, escalation-to-human rate), and cost/latency anomalies. These feed dashboards with alerting thresholds, so a regression pages someone rather than waiting for a customer complaint. When Azure announces a model version upgrade, I treat it as a change event and re-run the full eval suite *before* adopting it.

The trade-offs: online eval costs extra inference and you can only sample, so rare failure modes may hide — I supplement sampling with targeted monitors for high-risk intents. Implicit signals are noisy (a thumbs-down might be the user's fault, not the model's), so I trend them rather than reacting to single events. Response is staged: pin to the prior model/prompt version (this is why versioning everything matters), reproduce on the golden set, root-cause, fix, re-eval, and roll forward. The key architectural enabler is **version pinning and the ability to roll back** the model and prompt independently — without that, drift detection is academic because you can't react.

### Build vs. Buy

**Q31.** How do you make build-vs-buy calls for AI capabilities?

I apply the differentiation lens plus total cost of ownership. **Buy/adopt** (use a managed Azure service or SaaS) for undifferentiated, commoditizing capabilities — content safety, document intelligence, the foundation models themselves, vector search — where building in-house means reinventing a hardening curve Microsoft has already climbed. **Build** where the capability *is* your competitive edge: domain orchestration, proprietary data products, your specific evaluation and prompt assets, and the integration glue. The base model is the classic "buy" — almost no enterprise should train foundation models; the differentiation is in *how you apply* them to your data and workflow.

The trade-offs: buying accelerates time-to-value and offloads maintenance but creates dependency and recurring cost, and managed services may not fit edge requirements (data residency, custom logic). Building gives control and potential long-run cost advantage but carries hidden costs — security hardening, on-call, the MLOps treadmill of keeping pace with a field that moves monthly. My heuristic: buy the platform primitives, build the business logic, and revisit the line as services mature (capabilities that were "build" two years ago, like RAG plumbing, are increasingly "buy" via Foundry/Agent Service). The failure mode I warn against is teams lovingly hand-building infrastructure (vector DBs, eval frameworks) that Azure now offers off the shelf, burning roadmap on undifferentiated work.

**Q32.** When should an enterprise self-host open-weight models vs. use Azure OpenAI?

Azure OpenAI (managed) is my default: no infra to run, automatic scaling, integrated content safety, enterprise compliance, and access to frontier models. I reach for **self-hosted open-weight models** (on AKS with GPUs, or via Azure AI Foundry's managed/serverless model catalog) when there's a concrete driver: strict data residency/air-gap, the need to fine-tune deeply and own the weights, extreme cost optimization at massive sustained volume where owning GPUs beats per-token pricing, or specialized open models that outperform on a niche task.

The trade-offs are steep. Self-hosting means you own GPU capacity planning, scaling, patching, model serving (vLLM/Triton), and — critically — you must bolt on the content safety, monitoring, and guardrails that Azure OpenAI gives you for free. GPU scarcity and the fixed cost of reserved accelerators make the economics unforgiving unless utilization is high. Frontier open models also lag the best proprietary ones on hard reasoning. My opinionated stance: most enterprises overestimate the benefit and underestimate the operational drag of self-hosting; the sweet spot for open-weight is a well-understood, high-volume, narrow task with a data-residency or cost mandate. Azure AI Foundry's serverless model endpoints are a nice middle ground — open models without running the infrastructure yourself.

### Model Selection & Routing

**Q33.** How do you select the right model for a given use case?

I select against the use case's *requirements*, not the leaderboard. The axes: capability (does it pass my evals for this task?), context window (do I need long-document reasoning?), latency (interactive vs. batch), cost, modality (text, vision, audio), and compliance/region availability on Azure. The decisive step is running the candidate models through my **eval harness on representative data** — the only honest selection criterion is measured performance on *my* task, since generic benchmarks routinely mislead. I'll often pick the *smallest* model that passes, because that minimizes cost and latency.

The trade-offs: the strongest model is rarely the right default — it's slower and costlier, and over-provisioning capability is wasted money at scale. But under-provisioning quietly degrades the product. So I match tiers to traffic and revisit as new models ship (the frontier moves monthly; a model choice is a decision to revisit quarterly, not forever). I also weigh **lock-in**: choosing a model behind a stable gateway abstraction keeps the choice reversible. The opinionated take: anchor selection to evals and the smallest-sufficient principle, treat model choice as a living decision, and never let "the newest/biggest model" be the default just because it benchmarks well — your task's evals are the only benchmark that counts.

**Q34.** How do you handle the constant churn of new model versions on Azure?

Model churn is a fact of the platform, so I architect for it rather than fighting it. Everything points through the **gateway with a stable logical model name** (e.g., "chat-default") that maps to a concrete Azure OpenAI deployment/version in config — so swapping the underlying model is a config change, not a code change across dozens of consumers. I pin to specific model versions explicitly (never "latest" in production) and treat any version bump as a change event that runs the full eval suite before promotion, with canary rollout and instant rollback to the prior pinned version.

The trade-offs: pinning protects stability but you accrue "version debt" — Azure deprecates old versions on a schedule, so I track retirement dates and proactively migrate rather than getting force-upgraded. Auto-update is convenient but unacceptable for anything with quality or compliance stakes, because a silent base-model change can shift behavior, break prompts, or invalidate a fine-tune. The discipline I describe: stable abstraction + explicit version pinning + eval-gated, canaried upgrades + deprecation tracking. This converts a chaotic, vendor-driven churn into a managed, scheduled process the platform absorbs, so product teams experience stability even as the models beneath them turn over every few months.

### Vendor Lock-In Mitigation

**Q35.** How do you mitigate vendor lock-in while going all-in on Azure?

I distinguish strategic dependency from gratuitous coupling. I'm deliberately deep on Azure for the value (integration, compliance, support), but I insulate the *replaceable* pieces. The big lever is an **abstraction at the gateway and SDK layer**: my orchestration code talks to a provider-neutral interface (Semantic Kernel's connectors, or a thin internal client) rather than the raw Azure OpenAI SDK everywhere, so a different backend — another region, an open model on Foundry, or even another provider — is a connector swap. I keep prompts, eval datasets, and RAG indexes as **portable assets** I own, not locked in a proprietary format. Standard formats (OpenTelemetry for traces, open vector representations) reduce stickiness.

The trade-offs: abstraction has a cost — you forgo some provider-specific features and add an indirection layer that can lag the native SDK's newest capabilities. Pure portability ("multi-cloud everything") is usually over-engineering that taxes velocity for a switch you'll likely never make. So I aim for *reversibility of the expensive-to-redo parts* (data, prompts, evals, the consumer-facing contract) while pragmatically using rich Azure features behind that line. My honest position: embrace Azure's depth, but make sure your data, your prompt/eval IP, and your API contract are yours and portable — that's where real lock-in pain lives, not in which inference endpoint you call.

**Q36.** The business asks "what if we need to leave Azure OpenAI in 18 months?" How do you answer?

I answer with the exit-cost map. Three things are genuinely hard to move and three are easy. **Hard**: deeply integrated identity/compliance posture (Entra ID, Private Endpoints, CMK — re-architecting these is real work), any fine-tuned models (weights are tied to the base and not portable), and the operational muscle memory. **Easy/medium** if I've architected well: the inference endpoint itself (swap behind the gateway), prompts and eval datasets (provider-neutral assets I own), and the vector store (re-embed and reindex into an alternative). So my answer is: the model call is the *least* locked-in part if you've used a gateway abstraction; the data, identity, and compliance integration are the real switching costs.

The trade-offs I surface: I could minimize lock-in further with multi-provider abstraction now, but that slows us today for optionality we may never use — a poor trade unless leaving is a credible plan. So I recommend keeping the *reversible* assets portable (already my practice) and accepting strategic depth on Azure's identity/compliance stack, since those investments are valuable regardless of model provider. If leaving became real, the migration is: stand up the new endpoint behind the existing gateway, re-run evals to validate parity, re-embed the corpus, and cut over — weeks, not a rewrite, *because* the abstraction existed from day one. The cost of leaving is a function of architecture decisions made today.

### AI Gateway Patterns (APIM GenAI Policies)

**Q37.** Why put an AI gateway (APIM) in front of Azure OpenAI, and what does it buy you?

A direct app-to-Azure-OpenAI connection gives you no central control, so every cross-cutting concern gets reimplemented per team — or skipped. The gateway centralizes them. **APIM GenAI policies** specifically give: **token-based rate limiting** (`azure-openai-token-limit`) to enforce TPM quotas per consumer and prevent one team starving others; **token metric emission** (`azure-openai-emit-token-metric`) for cost attribution telemetry; **semantic caching** to cut cost/latency; **load balancing and circuit breaking** across multiple deployments/regions for reliability; and **content safety** policy integration. It also provides a single point for authentication (validate Entra ID tokens, mask backend keys via managed identity) and a stable API surface that decouples consumers from backend model changes.

The trade-offs: the gateway is an extra hop (small latency cost) and a potential single point of failure and bottleneck, so it must be deployed HA/multi-region and scaled — and it adds operational ownership. There's also a temptation to over-stuff logic into gateway policies, which become hard to test and version; I keep heavy orchestration in code and use the gateway for cross-cutting *policy*. The opinionated take: for any multi-team enterprise GenAI estate, the gateway is non-negotiable — it's the single place to enforce cost, security, reliability, and governance uniformly, and skipping it guarantees inconsistency and shadow AI.

**Q38.** Show how token-based rate limiting and quota work at the gateway.

APIM's token-limit policy meters by *tokens*, not just request count, which is what actually matters for Azure OpenAI capacity and cost. I attach it per APIM product/subscription so each tenant or team has its own TPM ceiling, and the gateway tracks consumption and returns 429 with retry guidance when a consumer exceeds its allotment — protecting both the shared backend and other tenants.

```xml
<azure-openai-token-limit
    counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
    tokens-per-minute="10000"
    estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
    remaining-tokens-header-name="x-tokens-remaining" />
<azure-openai-emit-token-metric namespace="genai">
    <dimension name="tenant" value="@(context.Subscription.Id)" />
</azure-openai-emit-token-metric>
```

The trade-offs: `estimate-prompt-tokens="true"` lets the gateway enforce limits *before* calling the backend (saving a wasted call) but the estimate isn't perfect, so I tune for safety; reconciling estimated vs. actual completion tokens requires emitting the metric and trueing-up in reporting. Setting limits too low throttles legitimate bursts and frustrates teams; too high defeats the protection and lets a runaway consumer rack up cost. So I set generous soft limits with alerting plus hard ceilings, and I tier limits by plan (free/standard/enterprise). The architectural value: quota and cost control become a declarative platform policy enforced uniformly, with per-tenant attribution emitted as metrics for chargeback — no per-app code required.

**Q39.** How do you implement multi-backend load balancing and failover in APIM for OpenAI?

I define a **backend pool** of multiple Azure OpenAI deployments — across regions and/or PTU+PAYG — with priorities and weights, and let APIM distribute traffic and fail over automatically. Priority 1 holds my PTU deployments (cheapest at utilization, latency-guaranteed); priority 2 holds PAYG/secondary-region deployments as spillover. A **circuit breaker** trips a backend out of the pool on repeated failures/429s and re-probes after a cooldown, so traffic shifts to healthy backends without per-app retry logic.

```text
Pool: openai-pool
  - east-us-ptu     priority 1  (PTU baseline)
  - west-us-ptu     priority 1  (PTU baseline, weighted)
  - east-us-paygo   priority 2  (burst / failover)
  circuit-breaker: trip on 3x 429/5xx in 30s, cooldown 60s
```

The trade-offs: load balancing across regions adds data-residency considerations (a request may be served from a different region — fine for many cases, not for data-sovereign ones, so I scope pools per residency boundary). Weighting and priority need tuning against real PTU capacity, or you either overflow to expensive PAYG too eagerly or saturate PTU and throttle. And failover only helps if secondaries have real headroom, so I capacity-plan them. The win: reliability and cost-optimization (PTU-first, PAYG-burst) become gateway configuration, giving consumers a single resilient endpoint while I manage a fleet of deployments behind it.

### Prompt & Version Management

**Q40.** How do you manage prompts as first-class, versioned artifacts?

Prompts are code — they change behavior, regress quietly, and need governance. I store them in **source control** (or a managed prompt registry / Azure AI Foundry prompt assets), versioned, reviewed via PR, and referenced by ID+version from the application rather than hardcoded inline. Each prompt version is bound to an eval result, so I know its measured quality before it ships. Promotion across dev/test/prod is a pipeline with the **eval suite as a gate**, and runtime telemetry records *which prompt version* produced each output for auditability and drift analysis. This lets me roll back a bad prompt instantly and A/B test prompt variants in production.

The trade-offs: a full prompt-management system (registry, versioning, eval binding) is overhead that small projects don't need — for a single app, prompts-in-repo with tests may suffice; the registry pays off at platform scale with many prompts and teams. Decoupling prompts from code enables non-engineers (PMs, domain experts) to iterate, which is powerful but risks ungoverned changes hitting production, so the eval gate and review are essential guardrails. My opinionated stance: treating prompts as ephemeral strings buried in code is the most common and most damaging anti-pattern — it makes regressions invisible and rollbacks impossible. Version them, eval them, and trace them like any other production artifact.

**Q41.** How do you safely A/B test prompts and roll out prompt changes in production?

I use feature-flag-style routing: a percentage of traffic gets the candidate prompt version while the rest stays on the control, with outcomes compared on quality (online eval scores, user signals like accept/regenerate/thumbs) and cost/latency. Routing happens at the orchestration layer keyed by the resolved prompt version, and the experiment is reversible instantly because the prompt is referenced by version, not hardcoded. I start with a small canary (1-5%), watch the metrics, and ramp only if the candidate is non-inferior or better.

The trade-offs: prompt outputs are non-deterministic, so A/B signals are noisy and need enough volume and time to reach significance — premature conclusions on small samples are a trap. Online quality eval costs extra inference, so I sample. There's also a consistency consideration: within a single user session I pin to one variant so the experience doesn't whipsaw. And not every change warrants an A/B — low-risk fixes can ship after passing offline evals, while behavior-shifting changes earn a live experiment. The principle: prompt changes go through the same canary-and-measure rigor as code deploys, enabled by prompts being versioned, routable, and traced — so I'm making rollout decisions on data, not on a demo or a hunch.

### Data Pipelines

**Q42.** Design the data pipeline that feeds a production RAG system.

The pipeline turns source content into a fresh, queryable, access-controlled index. Stages: **ingest** from sources (SharePoint, databases, Blob/ADLS) via connectors; **extract** structure and text with **Azure AI Document Intelligence** (handling PDFs, tables, images); **chunk** thoughtfully (semantic/structure-aware chunking, sized to the embedding model and overlap-tuned); **enrich** with metadata — tenant, ACLs, source, timestamp; **embed** via Azure OpenAI embeddings; and **index** into **Azure AI Search** with hybrid (keyword + vector) fields and the semantic ranker. **Azure AI Search's integrated vectorization / skillsets** can run much of this as a managed indexer pipeline. Updates run **incrementally** (change detection) so the index stays fresh without full reprocessing.

The trade-offs: chunking strategy is the highest-leverage and most-overlooked choice — bad chunking (too big, too small, splitting mid-context) silently caps retrieval quality no matter how good the model is. Freshness vs. cost: real-time indexing is expensive and complex; most use cases tolerate scheduled/incremental refresh. Embedding model choice locks the index — changing it means re-embedding everything. And **ACL/metadata at ingestion** is what makes security trimming possible later, so getting it right upstream is non-negotiable. My emphasis: retrieval quality is mostly a *data pipeline* problem, not a model problem — most "the LLM is dumb" complaints trace back to chunking, freshness, or missing metadata.

**Q43.** How do you keep embeddings and indexes fresh and consistent at scale?

I drive indexing from change events, not full rebuilds. Source systems emit changes (or I detect them via timestamps/CDC), enqueue them on **Service Bus / Event Grid**, and workers re-process only the affected documents — re-chunk, re-embed, upsert into AI Search. This keeps the index current at a sustainable cost. I version the **embedding model and chunking config** as part of the index's identity, because a change to either requires a coordinated re-embed; I handle that with a blue/green index swap (build the new index in parallel, validate via evals, then flip the alias) so queries never hit a half-rebuilt index.

The trade-offs: incremental updates are cheap but introduce consistency challenges — deletes and ACL changes must propagate promptly or you risk serving stale or unauthorized content (a security issue, not just a freshness one). Real-time freshness costs more and adds complexity; I match refresh cadence to the use case (a news bot needs minutes, a policy-manual bot tolerates daily). Re-embedding the whole corpus on a model change is expensive and slow, so embedding-model upgrades are deliberate, evaluated events, not casual ones. The architectural keys: event-driven incremental indexing for steady-state freshness, blue/green index swaps for breaking changes, and treating ACL/delete propagation with the same urgency as content updates because stale permissions in a RAG index are a data-leak vector.

### Team & Skill Strategy

**Q44.** How do you staff and skill a team to deliver enterprise AI?

I build for a blend, not a unicorn hunt. The core roles: an **AI/platform architect** (me) owning the reference architecture and guardrails; **application/.NET engineers** who build orchestration, integrations, and product features (the largest group — most GenAI work is software engineering, not ML research); **data engineers** for the RAG pipelines; an **MLOps/LLMOps** function owning evals, deployment, and monitoring; and **Responsible AI / security** partners. I deliberately *don't* over-index on data scientists — for applied GenAI on Azure, strong software engineers who understand prompting, RAG, and evals deliver more than a team of researchers.

The trade-offs: build vs. buy talent. Upskilling existing engineers (who know the domain and the codebase) is usually faster and stickier than hiring scarce, expensive AI specialists — so I invest in enablement (the platform SDK, paved-road templates, internal training, a community of practice) so any product team can build AI features safely without deep AI expertise. The risk is uneven skill leading to anti-patterns; the platform's golden path and guardrails mitigate that by making the right way the easy way. My opinionated view: the bottleneck in enterprise AI is rarely model expertise — it's software engineering discipline, data quality, and evaluation rigor, so I staff and train accordingly and let the platform abstract the hard model-handling parts.

**Q45.** How do you prevent shadow AI and drive adoption of the governed platform?

Shadow AI happens when the sanctioned path is slower or harder than going rogue. So my primary strategy is **make the paved road the fastest road**: a self-service onboarding under a day, a clean .NET SDK, ready-made RAG/agent templates, dashboards out of the box, and pre-cleared security/compliance. If using the platform saves a team weeks versus rolling their own, adoption is pull, not push. I pair this with guardrails — Entra ID conditional access and network controls that make unsanctioned external AI usage visible and, where policy requires, blocked — plus clear, *enabling* policy rather than a wall of "no."

The trade-offs: pure prohibition drives shadow AI deeper underground and breeds resentment; pure permissiveness yields ungoverned risk. I balance with carrots (great DX, fast onboarding, cost transparency that helps teams) and a few firm sticks (data-classification rules, blocked unsanctioned tools for sensitive data). I also run the platform as a product with a feedback loop, so teams' real needs shape the roadmap and they don't feel forced into something that doesn't fit. My stance: governance that relies only on enforcement fails; governance that wins makes compliance the easiest, most productive choice. Adoption is a developer-experience problem first and a policy problem second.

### Migration of Legacy Apps to AI

**Q46.** How do you approach AI-enabling a portfolio of legacy .NET applications?

I prioritize by value and feasibility, not by enthusiasm. I run a quick portfolio assessment scoring each app on AI opportunity (would a copilot, search, summarization, or automation materially help users?) against feasibility (data accessibility, API surface, integration risk). High-value/high-feasibility apps go first as lighthouse projects. Architecturally, I avoid rewrites: I add AI as a **new capability layer alongside** the legacy app — a sidecar service or API that the existing app calls — rather than threading model calls through brittle old code. The legacy app's data becomes a RAG source via the pipeline; the AI features ride the shared platform gateway for governance.

The trade-offs: strangler-fig (incremental, low-risk, slower) vs. rewrite (clean but expensive and risky). For legacy systems I almost always favor incremental augmentation — wrap, expose data via APIs, add AI at the edges — because rewriting a working system to "add AI" is rarely justified by the AI alone. Data access is usually the real blocker: legacy data is locked in old schemas or has no API, so a chunk of the work is *exposing* that data safely (with ACLs intact) before any AI is possible. My opinionated take: most legacy AI value comes from search, summarization, and assisted workflows layered on top — not from rebuilding the app — so I bias toward additive, governed, sidecar integration and let the shared platform carry the cross-cutting concerns.

**Q47.** A stakeholder wants to "add a chatbot" to a legacy CRM. How do you architect responsibly?

I reframe from "a chatbot" to "what user job are we accelerating," because an unfocused bot bolted onto a CRM usually disappoints. Assuming a grounded assistant over CRM data is the right answer, I build: a sidecar AI service authenticating via Entra ID with **OBO** so it only retrieves records the *signed-in user* can see (preserving CRM permissions — critical, or the bot becomes a data-leak engine); RAG over the CRM data through the pipeline with ACL metadata; the shared gateway for safety, cost, and quota; and a tightly-scoped tool set if it needs to *act* on records (with validation and confirmation on writes). The UX is embedded in the CRM, streams responses, cites sources, and degrades gracefully.

The trade-offs: read-only assistant (safe, lower value) vs. action-taking agent (higher value, higher risk — a hallucinated update to a customer record is a real harm), so writes get human confirmation and idempotency. Permissions are the make-or-break: easy to build a bot that ignores CRM ACLs and surfaces records a user shouldn't see, so OBO/security-trimming is non-negotiable. I also set expectations on accuracy via grounding and evals, and instrument feedback to improve it. The responsible architecture inherits the legacy app's existing access model rather than bypassing it, layers governance through the platform, and starts read-only-with-citations before earning the right to take actions.

### SLAs for Non-Deterministic Systems

**Q48.** How do you define SLAs and SLOs for a non-deterministic AI system?

I split what I *can* commit to from what I can only target. **SLAs** (contractual) cover the deterministic, measurable properties: availability of the endpoint, latency percentiles (p50/p95 first-token and total), and throughput — these compose from Azure's underlying SLAs plus my architecture (multi-region failover, PTU guarantees). What I do **not** put in a hard SLA is answer *correctness*, because output is non-deterministic and "correct" is fuzzy — instead I express quality as **SLOs**: target groundedness/relevance scores, a maximum harmful-content rate, and user-satisfaction thresholds, measured continuously via evals and tracked as objectives, not guarantees.

The trade-offs: customers want a correctness guarantee, and I have to educate that a probabilistic system can't promise per-response accuracy the way a CRUD API promises a correct read — over-promising correctness is a legal and trust landmine. So I commit hard on availability/latency/safety-process, and commit to *measured, monitored* quality targets with transparency and a remediation process when they slip. Latency SLAs themselves require care: streaming changes the meaning (first-token vs. completion), and model/region failover can shift latency, so I define the metric precisely. The honest architect's framing: guarantee the operational envelope, target and continuously evidence the quality, and never sign an SLA that treats a non-deterministic model like a deterministic function.

**Q49.** How do you guarantee latency SLAs when model latency is variable?

Model latency varies with load, prompt/output length, and model choice, so I engineer the controllable factors. **PTU** gives guaranteed throughput and far more predictable latency than shared PAYG (which can queue under contention) — so latency-sensitive paths run on PTU. I cap output length (the dominant driver of total latency), use **streaming** so the committed metric is *time-to-first-token* (consistently fast) rather than total completion, route simple requests to faster small models, and serve cache hits instantly. The gateway's failover keeps a slow/throttled backend from blowing the percentile by shifting to a healthier one, and I enforce timeouts with graceful degradation rather than letting a tail request hang.

The trade-offs: PTU buys predictability but costs money to reserve; capping output length protects latency but can truncate complex answers, so I tune per use case. Streaming lets me commit an aggressive first-token SLA but total-completion time stays variable — so I'm precise about *which* latency I'm guaranteeing. Routing to faster models trades some quality for speed. The architectural answer: you don't make the model deterministic, you make latency *predictable* through provisioned capacity, output control, streaming, caching, and failover — and you commit SLAs on the metric you've actually engineered to be stable (first-token, p95) rather than on raw end-to-end generation time you can't fully control.

### Capacity Planning

**Q50.** Walk me through capacity planning for a GenAI platform.

Capacity in GenAI means tokens-per-minute, not just CPUs. I start from demand: forecast requests per use case, average prompt + completion tokens per request, and the temporal shape (peak vs. average, business-hours spikes). Multiplying gives required TPM and peak concurrency. I then map that to backend capacity — sizing **PTU** to cover the predictable baseline (provisioned for, say, p90 of steady demand) and reserving **PAYG spillover** plus multi-region deployments for the peaks above PTU. The stateless orchestration tier I size for concurrency and autoscale; the real constraint is the model quota, so I validate that my deployments' combined TPM/RPM headroom covers forecast peaks with margin.

The trade-offs: over-provision PTU and you pay for idle capacity; under-provision and you throttle users or overflow expensively to PAYG. Forecasts are uncertain — especially for new AI features where adoption is unknown — so I start PAYG, measure real traffic for several weeks, then commit PTU to the proven baseline, re-evaluating quarterly as usage grows. **GPU and PTU scarcity** mean capacity isn't always instantly available, so for large commitments I plan and reserve ahead rather than assuming elastic supply. I also reduce demand through caching, routing, and prompt trimming, which directly lowers the capacity I must buy. The discipline: model demand in tokens, provision a PTU baseline with PAYG burst, leave headroom, measure relentlessly, and treat capacity as a continuously-tuned commitment, not a one-time sizing.

**Q16.** How do you do capacity and cost estimation for an AI workload?

Estimate from usage: requests/day, avg input+output tokens/request, model price/1k tokens. Daily cost = requests x (in_tokens x in_price + out_tokens x out_price)/1000. Then decide PTU vs PAYG: PTUs give reserved throughput (tokens/min) at fixed monthly cost — worthwhile when sustained utilization is high and you need predictable latency; PAYG suits spiky/low volume. Compute required TPM from peak RPS x tokens/request to size PTUs or check PAYG quota. Add costs for embeddings (one-time ingest + per-query), Azure AI Search tier, Cosmos RU/s, Redis, compute (Container Apps), and egress. Apply caching hit-rate to reduce model calls. Example: 200k req/day, 1.5k in + 0.5k out tokens, GPT-4o-class -> size accordingly, then validate against quotas. Trade-offs: PTU (predictable, risk of over/under-provision) vs PAYG (elastic, variable, throttle risk); reserved capacity discounts vs flexibility. Build a model with peak vs average and a buffer (~20-30%).
